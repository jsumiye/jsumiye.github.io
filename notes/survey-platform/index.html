A survey stack for K-12, from builder to PDF

May 2025

A client sent a folder of .qst files and asked if the new site could read them. I had not heard of the format. The files held years of questions, school names, and response data from a system that was not coming with us. The job was to keep that history and give staff a builder that did not feel like 2009.

This is private work for K-12 survey operations. I cannot link a demo. I can walk through how a survey moves through the system, because that is the part I still think about.

Three surfaces share one database. The builder writes a survey. The taker writes responses. Reporting reads both and draws charts that have to line up across years.

The table that runs the week

Most of the operational work happens on one screen. Category, author, status. School, season, year. A count of responses. Opens-at and expires-at, which sound like timestamps until a teacher needs a survey to go live at midnight in a state you do not live in.

Visibility is private, unlisted, or public, shown as a small badge rather than a settings maze. Sharing is a slug. Copy the link, or the UI falls back if the clipboard call fails, because school machines are not always in the mood.

DataTables handles sort, page, and a few custom filters. I care less about the library than about the fact that a person can find last fall's postgrad survey for a Texas high school without asking me.

Reading a format that wanted to stay

The QST importer is the unglamorous center of the project. A file comes in, sometimes with a CSV or DAT sibling. Metadata fields turn on as the parser learns what it has. Get the school name wrong and you have invented a campus. Get the question order wrong and a five-year comparison chart lies.

I treat the parser as a pipeline with boring checkpoints, not a clever one-liner. 

function ingestLegacySurvey(file, extras = {}) {
  const qst = parseQst(file);
  const rows = extras.csv ? parseCsv(extras.csv) : [];

  assertQuestions(qst.questions);
  if (rows.length) assertSchema(qst, rows);

  return {
    title: qst.title,
    questions: qst.questions,
    choices: qst.choices,
    responses: rows,
  };
}

That is the shape, not a dump of the production file. The real parser is pickier. Matrix questions flatten into rows. Choice ids have to survive a round trip into SurveyJS so a teacher can edit an imported survey without the toolbox offering a hundred question types they were never supposed to see. The palette is curated on purpose.

The database has to survive a school year

Features arrived after the first schema. I write migrations that keep old rows valid. A new opens_at column cannot blank out surveys that were already in the wild. Student-adjacent data means I do not concatenate SQL, even when the table UI makes it tempting. 

$stmt = $pdo->prepare(
  'SELECT id, school_name, response_count
     FROM surveys
    WHERE author_id = :author
      AND status = :status
    ORDER BY updated_at DESC'
);
$stmt->execute([
  ':author' => $authorId,
  ':status' => $status,
]);

State-changing posts check a CSRF token in a cookie and in the form. Roles decide who sees another school's reports. Input is validated before it hits the CSV replace path, because bulk upload is where people paste the wrong year.

Reports are the product

Staff do not brag about a builder. They email a PDF. The report pipeline detects questions that line up across surveys, flattens matrix rows, and feeds html2canvas into jsPDF so the output is a letter-size document with a header and footer that stay put when the chart count gets rude.

Templates split into page templates and a more advanced set. Duplicate and rename exist because nobody wants to rebuild last year's layout from a blank page. Comparison charts are the feature I would keep if I could keep only one. A single-year bar is a slide. A three-year overlay is an argument.

What I would still change

Scheduling across time zones is more interesting than it looks, and I am not done being suspicious of it. The importer could explain itself better when a QST file is almost valid. The rest of the stack is the kind of PHP, MySQL, and JavaScript that has to be boring on purpose. Schools will still be opening these surveys next fall.
